1 · The problem: fans & bearings

A big power-plant fan spins on ball bearings. Bearings get small cracks that grow slowly. If we notice early, we can fix it cheaply instead of suffering a sudden breakdown.

A coal power plant has PA (Primary Air) fans. They blow air that carries coal powder into the boiler. If one stops unexpectedly, the boiler loses fuel and the plant can trip — expensive and rushed.

The part that most often fails is the ball bearing: a ring of steel balls that lets the shaft spin smoothly.

Four parts can be damaged:

  • Outer race — the outer ring. It stays still.
  • Inner race — the inner ring. It spins with the shaft.
  • Balls — the rolling steel balls.
  • Cage — holds the balls apart.

Our system recognises 4 classes: Normal, Inner race, Outer race, Ball fault.

A bearing failing is like a pothole forming on a road. It starts as a tiny crack, every car makes it a bit worse, and eventually a wheel breaks. Spotting the crack early means a cheap patch instead of an accident.

The data we use: CWRU

We do not have recordings from a real PA fan. We use the famous CWRU bearing dataset (Case Western Reserve University). Researchers put a 6205 bearing in a 2 HP motor, deliberately made pits of 0.007, 0.014, 0.021 and 0.028 inch, and recorded the vibration at 4 loads (0–3 HP, ~1797–1730 RPM).

CWRU is a laboratory test rig, not a fan. The PA fan is the target application. Everything works on CWRU; it has not been validated on a real fan.

2 · Vibration, signals & sampling

A sensor measures shaking many thousands of times per second. That list of measurements is the "signal". You must measure fast enough, or you see fake slow waves (aliasing).

An accelerometer glued to the bearing housing measures how hard it shakes. It does not record a smooth curve — it takes a sample (one number) at regular moments.

  • Frequency = how many times per second something repeats, in Hertz (Hz). 50 Hz = 50 times per second.
  • Sampling rate (fs) = how many samples per second the sensor takes. CWRU fault data: 12,000 per second (12 kHz). CWRU healthy data: 48 kHz.
  • RPM = revolutions per minute. 1750 RPM ÷ 60 = 29.17 turns per second (29.17 Hz).
Sampling a wave

Nyquist rule: to see a frequency f you need a sampling rate of more than 2·f. At 12 kHz we can see up to 6 kHz — enough for bearing faults.

Resampling: why the healthy data had to be "slowed down"

Healthy CWRU files are recorded at 48 kHz, faulty ones at 12 kHz. If we feed the model different rates, it would learn "48 kHz = healthy" — a cheat! So we resample everything to 12 kHz. We first remove frequencies above 6 kHz with a filter (anti-aliasing), then keep every 4th sample. The project verified the 48 kHz assumption from the data itself: the shaft-speed peak landed exactly at RPM÷60 (e.g. 30.0 Hz found vs 29.93 Hz expected).

48,000 samples per second → keep 1 of every 4 → 12,000 per second. A 5-second healthy recording with 243,938 samples becomes ~60,985 samples.

3 · Why a damaged bearing "knocks"

Every time a ball rolls over a crack it makes a tiny "knock". How often it knocks depends on WHERE the crack is — that's how we can tell faults apart.

The bearing geometry fixes how many knocks happen per shaft turn. These are the defect frequency multipliers for the 6205 bearing (from the CWRU website):

Crack locationNameKnocks per shaft turn
Outer raceBPFO (Ball Pass Frequency, Outer)3.5848
Inner raceBPFI (Ball Pass Frequency, Inner)5.4152
BallBSF (Ball Spin Frequency)4.7135
CageFTF (Fundamental Train Frequency)0.39828
Shaft at 1750 RPM → 1750 / 60 = 29.17 turns/s. Outer-race crack: 29.17 × 3.5848 = 104.6 knocks per second (104.6 Hz). Inner-race crack: 29.17 × 5.4152 = 157.9 Hz.
Hear it with your eyes: simulated vibration

Notice: outer race knocks are evenly strong (the crack sits still, always in the loaded zone). Inner race knocks get louder and quieter once per shaft turn (the crack travels in and out of the load zone). Ball faults are weak and irregular — the hardest to detect.

4 · Seeing frequencies: the FFT

The FFT takes a wiggly signal and tells you which frequencies are inside it and how strong each one is — like a recipe listing ingredients.
Hearing an orchestra chord, a trained ear can say "there's a C, an E and a G". The FFT (Fast Fourier Transform) does that for any signal: it breaks it into pure tones and gives the strength of each. The result is called a spectrum.
Mix two tones, then look at the spectrum
Top: the signal over 1 second (what the sensor sees). Bottom: its spectrum (what the FFT sees). The two tall bars sit exactly at your two tone frequencies.

In the project, frequency-domain features include the spectral centroid (the "centre of gravity" of the spectrum), the dominant frequency (tallest bar) and the share of energy in 8 bands from 0 to 6 kHz (0–750 Hz, 750–1500 Hz, …).

5 · Envelope analysis: finding the knock rate

Each knock makes the bearing housing "ring" at a high pitch (thousands of Hz). The envelope traces the outline of these rings, and its spectrum shows how often the knocks happen (~100 Hz).

A plain FFT of a faulty bearing is messy: each knock excites a resonance at ~2–5 kHz, so the energy is high up, not at 105 Hz. The classic trick (used in industry for decades) has 3 steps:

  1. Band-pass filter 2,000–5,500 Hz → keep only the ringing.
  2. Envelope (Hilbert transform) → trace the outline of the rings, like drawing a line over the tops of waves.
  3. FFT of the envelope → a clear peak at the knock rate (BPFO, BPFI or BSF) and its multiples.
The three steps on a simulated faulty bearing

From the envelope spectrum the project computes 10 envelope features: the peak height near BPFO, BPFI and BSF (×1, ×2, ×3) and near the shaft speed, each divided by the spectrum's background level. A value near 1–2 means "nothing special"; above 5 means "a clear repeating knock".

In the real CWRU data some damaged bearings show no clear peak (for example outer race 0.014″: BPFO ratio 1.6, same as a healthy bearing). This becomes very important in chapter 12.

6 · Cutting signals into windows

We cut each recording into short overlapping pieces (2048 samples ≈ 0.17 s). Each piece becomes one row of numbers — one example for the model.

A 10-second recording is too long to describe with one set of numbers, and one recording is just one example. So we slice it into windows of 2048 samples (= 0.17 s at 12 kHz ≈ 5 shaft turns), each overlapping the previous by 50%.

How many windows?
10.1 s × 12,000 = 121,200 samples. Step = 2048 × (1 − 0.5) = 1024. Windows = 1 + ⌊(121,200 − 2048) / 1024⌋ = 117. That's exactly what the project produced for most CWRU recordings.

7 · Features: turning waves into numbers

A model cannot look at a wave. We describe each window with 37 numbers ("features") — how big, how spiky, which frequencies, how strong each knock rate is, and which knock dominates.
A doctor doesn't diagnose from your whole body at once; they measure temperature, blood pressure, heart rate. Features are the vital signs of a vibration window.

The 37 features in 4 groups

GroupFeaturesPlain meaning
Time (11)mean, std, RMS, min, max, peak-to-peak, kurtosis, skewness, crest, impulse, shape factorHow big is the shaking, and how spiky is it?
Frequency (10)spectral centroid, dominant frequency, energy share in 8 bandsIs the vibration high-pitched or low-pitched?
Envelope (10)peaks at BPFO/BPFI/BSF ×1,2,3 and shaft ×1Is there a repeating knock at a fault's rate?
Knock ratios (6)share of BPFO / BPFI / BSF in the total knock energy, and log-ratios between themWhich knock dominates? (works whatever the overall loudness)
The fault-type model deliberately does not use the 6 absolute-size features (mean, std, RMS, min, max, peak-to-peak). Loudness depends on the individual bearing, sensor and machine, and with them the model learned to "recognise bearings" instead of faults (chapter 12). They are still computed and shown, and the size model uses them, because bigger cracks do knock harder.

Two of the most useful ideas:

  • RMS (root mean square) = the typical size of the shaking. Square every value, average, take the square root.
  • Kurtosis = "how spiky". Smooth random noise has kurtosis ≈ 3. Sharp knocks push it far higher (CWRU outer-race windows median 7.75; one inner-race bearing over 20).
Compute features yourself
Smooth wave: RMS 0.50, crest factor 1.41, kurtosis 1.5. Same wave plus one big spike of 3.0: crest factor jumps to ~3.4 and kurtosis to ~7. One knock changes the "spikiness" numbers dramatically even though the average barely moves.

8 · What machine learning is

A model is a function: numbers in → answer out. "Training" means finding the function's rules automatically from many examples whose answers we already know.

Normally a programmer writes rules: if kurtosis > 5 then "fault". With 31 features and 4 classes, nobody can hand-write good rules. Machine learning lets the computer discover the rules from examples.

Teaching a child to recognise dogs: you don't explain fur length thresholds. You show many pictures labelled "dog" / "not dog" and the child figures out the pattern. That's supervised learning — learning with an answer key.

The three stages

1 · Training data
5,577 windows. Each has 31 feature numbers and the true label (e.g. "Outer race", size 0.014″). This is the homework with the answer key.
2 · Training
The algorithm adjusts its internal rules until its answers match the labels on the training data. Takes ~seconds to minutes.
3 · Testing
An exam on examples the model never saw. Only this score tells you if it really learned, rather than memorised.

Two kinds of learning in this project

Supervised learningReinforcement learning
Used forDiagnosis: Random Forests (fault type, fault size)Decisions: the PPO maintenance agent

Phase 1 vs Phase 2

Phase 1 — training (run once by the scripts, minutes): create the models. Phase 2 — runtime (each "Analyze" click, under 1 second): apply the frozen models to a new recording. Nothing learns at runtime.

9 · Decision trees & how they are trained

A decision tree is a flowchart of yes/no questions. Training picks, at each step, the question that best separates the classes — measured by "Gini impurity".
Is kurtosis > 4.2?
├── no  → Is BPFI peak > 3.8?
│         ├── no  → "Normal"
│         └── yes → "Inner race"
└── yes → Is BPFO peak > 5.0?
          ├── yes → "Outer race"
          └── no  → "Ball"

To predict, a window walks down from the top, answering questions, until it reaches a leaf with an answer.

How does the computer choose the questions?

It measures how mixed a group is with Gini impurity:

Gini = 1 − (p₁² + p₂² + …)

where p₁, p₂… are the shares of each class in the group.

A group with 5 healthy + 5 faulty: Gini = 1 − (0.5² + 0.5²) = 0.5 (very mixed). A group with 10 faulty: Gini = 1 − 1² = 0 (pure). Training wants pure groups.

For every feature and every possible threshold, the computer tries the split and computes the weighted Gini of the two resulting groups. It keeps the split with the lowest weighted Gini, then repeats inside each group — until groups are pure (or too small).

Find the best question yourself

12 windows: kurtosis values. Green = healthy, red = faulty. Move the threshold: "Is kurtosis > threshold?"

That's literally what scikit-learn does inside DecisionTreeClassifier.fit() — just with 31 features, thousands of windows and 4 classes, very fast in C code.

The problem with one tree

A single deep tree can memorise the training data, including noise (overfitting). It answers perfectly on homework and badly on the exam. The fix: many different trees that vote. →

10 · Random Forest training, inside out

A Random Forest trains 100 slightly different trees (each on a random resample of the data, each split choosing from a random subset of features) and lets them vote. Confidence = share of votes.
Ask one friend for directions and they might be wrong. Ask 100 friends who each know slightly different parts of town, and go where most of them point. Their individual mistakes cancel out.

Step 1 — Bootstrap: each tree gets its own random dataset

For each tree, draw N windows with replacement from the N training windows. Some windows appear twice or three times, about 37% don't appear at all ("out-of-bag").

Draw a bootstrap sample

Step 2 — Random features at every split

At each question, a tree may only look at a random subset of the features — by default √31 ≈ 5 of the 31. So one tree may build its question on kurtosis, another on the BPFO peak. This makes the trees different from each other, which is what makes voting useful.

Step 3 — Grow each tree (chapter 9) until leaves are pure

Step 4 — Predict by voting

A new window goes down all 100 trees. Each tree says a class. Confidence = share of trees that agree (technically scikit-learn averages each tree's leaf probabilities; with fully grown trees that equals the vote share). For a whole recording we average over all its windows.

A mini forest of 9 trees votes on one window
Each tree only "sees" some features (random subsets) and learned slightly different thresholds (bootstrap). Try BPFO = 10 with kurtosis 8; then a borderline case like BPFO 4.5.

Class balance

Only 4 healthy recordings (409 windows) vs ~1,400–1,900 windows for each fault. Without care, the forest would "ignore" Normal. With class_weight="balanced" each Normal window counts more: weight = total ÷ (4 × class count) = 5577 ÷ (4 × 409) ≈ 3.4, while a Ball window counts 5577 ÷ (4 × 1878) ≈ 0.74.

The project's training in pseudo-code

for tree in 1..100:
    data = bootstrap_sample(5577 windows)          # step 1
    grow(tree, data):                               # steps 2-3
        if node is pure: make leaf
        features = random 5 of 31
        best = split with lowest weighted Gini over those features
        grow(left), grow(right)
predict(window) = average of the 100 trees' votes  # step 4

11 · Severity & health rules

A second Random Forest (trained only on faulty windows) estimates the crack size. Simple written rules then turn class + confidence + size + load into Healthy / Warning / Critical.

Why a separate model? It would be wrong to say "the model is 60% sure, so the damage is 60% bad" — confidence is not severity. The severity RF learns size (0.007 / 0.014 / 0.021 / 0.028 inch) from the same 31 features, using only faulty windows.

12 · Honest testing & metrics

How you split data into "study" and "exam" decides how honest the score is. On bearings it has seen at other loads the model is ~99.7% right; on bearings it has never seen, the ML model alone types 58% of recordings correctly — and 75% when combined with a physics knock check.

Leakage: when the exam leaks into the homework

Windows overlap by 50%, so neighbouring windows are near-copies. If we shuffle all windows randomly into train/test, the exam contains near-copies of homework → fake 100%.

Three ways to split — see what the model is tested on
trainingtesting

The key discovery of this project: CWRU recorded each damaged bearing (e.g. "inner race 0.014″") at all four loads. In leave-one-load-out, the test bearing at 3 HP is the same physical bearing the model trained on at 0–2 HP. The model can partly recognise that bearing rather than the fault in general. Holding out whole bearings (all loads of one size) is the honest test for a new machine.

Metrics in plain words

Suppose we test 100 windows for "is it faulty?":

Precision, recall and F1
  • Accuracy — share of all answers that were right.
  • Precision — "when it says fault, how often is it right?" (few false alarms)
  • Recall — "of all real faults, how many did it catch?" (few misses)
  • F1 — one number balancing both (harmonic mean). Macro-F1 = average F1 over the 4 classes, so the small Normal class counts as much as the others.

The confusion matrix

A table: rows = truth, columns = prediction. The diagonal = correct. This is the project's matrix on unseen bearings (share of windows):

How the model was improved — two opinions are better than one

1 · Better clues. The first model leaned on how loud the vibration was — which differs from bearing to bearing — so it partly memorised bearings. Removing the 6 loudness features and adding 6 "which knock dominates" ratios lifted unseen-bearing accuracy from 37% to 58%.

2 · A physics cross-check (no training): if one fault family's knock peak is at least 5× the background and at least 1.5× the next family, that fault is named. On CWRU it spoke on 19 of 48 recordings and was right 19 out of 19 times; when there is no clear knock it stays silent and the ML model decides. Combined: 75%. If the two opinions disagree, the dashboard says so and flags low confidence.

A vibration expert does the same: first look for the textbook knock at the defect frequency; only if there is none, judge from the overall "feel" of the signal.
To keep this honest, the improvement was chosen with a rule fixed before looking at the 4 never-used recordings, which were checked only afterwards. Some CWRU recordings simply have no clear knock signature (outer race 0.014″, inner race 0.028″, most ball faults), so no method gets everything right. Crack-size estimates still do not transfer to an unseen fault type (0.128 macro-F1, below the ~0.25 of guessing).

2 · ONNX: a universal model format

Python's scikit-learn models are saved with joblib (a Python-only "pickle"). ONNX is a neutral file format describing the trees as a graph of operations, runnable by the small onnxruntime library on Windows, Linux, Raspberry Pi, phones…

Parity check: ONNX uses 32-bit numbers, scikit-learn 64-bit; in rare cases a value sitting exactly on a threshold could flip. We checked all 5,577 windows: 0 differences (max probability difference < 0.000001).

14 · What reinforcement learning is

An "agent" learns what to DO by trial and error: it tries actions in an environment, receives rewards or penalties, and gradually prefers actions that lead to more total reward.
Training a dog: it does something, you give a treat or say "no". Nobody shows the dog the correct answer for every situation — it figures out from rewards which behaviour pays off. Or: playing a video game thousands of times until you know which moves win.

The vocabulary

Why not supervised learning here? There is no answer key saying "on day 37 the correct action was inspect". Only the consequences tell us what was good — sometimes much later (a breakdown 20 days after a bad decision). RL is designed for exactly that.

15 · Our simulator — play it!

We built a "maintenance game" where a hidden crack grows day by day. The agent only sees noisy diagnoses — sampled from the real model's mistakes on unseen bearings — and must decide what to do.

The hidden truth (agent can't see it)

  • 35% of games start with a small fault; otherwise a healthy bearing gets a fault with 1.5% chance per day.
  • Degradation grows from 0 to 1, faster at higher load and faster as it gets worse.
  • Daily breakdown chance = 0.3 × degradation⁵ × load factor. At 0.5 → ~1% per day; at 0.75 → ~7%; at 1.0 → ~30%.

What the agent sees

Each measurement is sampled from the real Random Forest's out-of-fold predictions on unseen bearings. So if the real model confuses outer-race faults with ball faults 87% of the time, so does the simulator. The agent trains with realistic mistakes.

Actions and their (illustrative) costs

Monitor more frequently−1 to switch on, −0.2/day; measurements daily instead of weekly and more accurate
Schedule inspection−4 and 1 day downtime; reveals the true degradation
Reduce operating load+6 per day; wear and breakdown risk halved
Stop for maintenance−30 and 3 days downtime; new bearing. Extra −15 if the bearing was actually healthy
Breakdown−300 and the game ends (all future production lost)
Safety−5 per day of running while truly critical (degradation ≥ 0.75)
You are the maintenance engineer (simplified version, 90 days, load 2 HP)
The hidden degradation is revealed at the end of the game.
Every cost, wear rate and failure probability is an illustrative assumption (in config.yaml), not plant data. The game teaches the method; it is not a validated maintenance policy.

16 · Neural networks & gradient descent

A neural network is layers of simple calculators with adjustable "knobs" (weights). Training nudges every knob a tiny bit in the direction that reduces the error — that's gradient descent.

The PPO agent's brain is a small neural network (it is the only neural network in this project — the Random Forests are not neural networks):

  • Input: the 16 observation numbers.
  • Each neuron multiplies its inputs by weights, adds them up, then applies a simple bend (tanh). Example: 0.8×0.5 + 0.2×(−1.0) + 0.1 = 0.3 → tanh(0.3) = 0.29.
  • Two hidden layers of 64 neurons find useful combinations ("fault diagnosed AND high load").
  • Output: 5 scores, turned into 5 probabilities by softmax.
Softmax: scores → probabilities
Softmax: p = e^score ÷ sum of all e^scores. Higher score → higher probability; all probabilities add up to 100%. Raise one score by 1 and its probability multiplies by e ≈ 2.7 relative to the others.

Gradient descent: how the knobs get tuned

Training defines a loss = "how bad the network currently is". For each weight we compute the gradient (slope): if I increase this weight a little, does the loss go up or down? Then we move each weight a small step downhill. The step size is the learning rate (our PPO: 0.0003).

Walking down a foggy mountain: you can't see the valley, but you can feel which way the ground slopes under your feet. Take a small step downhill, feel again, repeat.
Gradient descent on one knob

Our network has about 10,000 weights: (16×64 + 64) + (64×64 + 64) + (64×5 + 5) for the policy, plus a similar "value" network. Gradient descent adjusts all of them at once, using calculus (backpropagation) to get every slope efficiently.

17 · How PPO training works inside

PPO plays many days in the simulator, checks which actions turned out better than expected, makes those a bit more likely and worse ones a bit less likely — but never changes too much at once (the "clip").

The training loop (what happened during the ~3–7 minutes per seed)

  1. Play: 8 copies of the simulator × 512 days = 4,096 decisions, choosing actions by the current probabilities (so it explores).
  2. Score: for each decision compute the return (the rewards that followed, future ones discounted) and the advantage = return − what the value network expected.
  3. Learn: 10 passes over the 4,096 decisions in mini-batches of 256: raise probabilities of positive-advantage actions, lower negative ones, with clipping; improve the value network's predictions.
  4. Repeat ~122 times = 500,000 simulated days (~2,900 games).

Discounting: money today vs money later

Future rewards count slightly less: a reward n days ahead is multiplied by γⁿ (γ = 0.99). This makes the agent care about the next ~100 days most.

How much is +10 worth if it comes later?

Advantage: "better or worse than expected?"

The value network predicts that from today's situation the future is worth 1,200. The agent chose "Inspect" and the actual (discounted) outcome was 1,260. Advantage = +60 → make "Inspect" a bit more likely in situations like this. If the outcome had been 1,100, advantage = −100 → make it less likely.

The clip: small, safe steps

One lucky game shouldn't rewrite the whole strategy. PPO limits each update so the new probability of an action is at most ±20% different from the old one (ratio between 0.8 and 1.2). That's the "Proximal" (= nearby) in Proximal Policy Optimization.

Watch a policy learn in one situation: "Warning — fault diagnosed, size 0.007″"
Hidden "true" value of each action in this toy situation: Stop 5 · Monitor 3 · Continue 2 · Inspect 1 · Reduce 0. The agent doesn't know them; it only sees noisy outcomes. Turn off the clip and raise the learning rate to see why big jumps are risky.

Two more ingredients

  • Entropy bonus (0.01): a small reward for staying a bit random, so it keeps exploring instead of locking in early.
  • Reward normalisation: rewards range from +10 to −300; they are rescaled during training so updates stay stable.

On the same games, the shielded agent beat the rules by +62 per game (95% interval +42 to +81) and the unshielded one by +86. All three training seeds (three random starting points — same code, different luck) beat the rules: 1,622 / 1,571 / 1,549. What the agent learned can surprise you: crack-size estimates are unreliable on unseen bearings, so in the simulator most "Critical" readings are false alarms. The agent learned to treat "Critical" alone as weak evidence and often keeps running, relying on other clues — with zero breakdowns in simulation. On a real Critical reading that is not acceptable advice, so the project adds a safety shield: on a Critical rating the agent may not recommend "Continue" or "Monitor", and the rule-based action is used instead (the dashboard says when this happens). It costs about 1.5% simulated reward and still has zero breakdowns. RL optimises exactly the game you give it, including its blind spots — a shield is a common way to add hard safety rules on top.

18 · "What if we keep running?" — Monte Carlo simulation

We can't know the future, so we simulate it 300 times from the current diagnosis and count how often the bearing breaks within 7, 30, 90 and 180 days.

The diagnosis doesn't reveal the exact hidden degradation, so each simulated future first draws a plausible starting state: fault type from the class probabilities, degradation from the estimated size band (e.g. 0.014″ → between 0.25 and 0.5). Then it runs the simulator with "continue" every day.

Outer race 0.014″ at 2 HP: in 300 simulated futures, 10% broke within 7 days, 98% within 30 days, 100% within 90 days. This is a simulated estimate based on the assumed wear rates — "Monte Carlo" just means "estimate by repeated random simulation", like rolling dice many times to learn the odds.

19 · The AI assistant & its guard

A large language model (Gemini) writes the explanations, but it only receives the analysis report as data and strict rules. A "guard" then checks every number in its answer against the report.

What a large language model (LLM) is

An LLM is a huge neural network trained on enormous amounts of text to predict the next word. Doing that well makes it able to explain, summarise and answer questions fluently.

It's like a very well-read assistant with no access to your machine. Ask "how is bearing 3?" with no data, and it may confidently make something up — this is called hallucination. Hand it the inspection report first and ask it to only use that, and it becomes useful and trustworthy.

How the project keeps it honest (grounding)

  1. Only the report: the AnalysisReport is converted to JSON (diagnosis, probabilities, health, envelope peaks, top features vs healthy, RL action, simulated risk, caveats). Raw signals are not sent.
  2. Strict system prompt: "use only the report; never invent readings, predictions or actions; say 'not available' if missing; always call simulated numbers simulated".
  3. Real simulations for what-if questions: "What if we reduce the load?" actually runs the simulator, then gives the result to the model.
  4. Number guard: every number in the answer is checked against the report; unmatched ones are shown as a warning.
  5. Offline fallback: with no API key or no internet, 7 standard questions are answered from templates filled with the real report values.

21 · Limitations & honesty

  
Know what the numbers do and don't mean before trusting them on a real fan.

  
    
    
  1. Lab data, not a fan. CWRU is a 2 HP motor rig. A real PA fan has different speeds, structure, sensor placement and noise. Retraining and validation on fan data would be needed.
    2. 
    
  2. Artificial faults. CWRU damage was machined, not grown naturally. Each fault type and size is one physical bearing.
    3. 
    
  3. Seen vs unseen bearings. 99.9% when the bearing was seen at another load; 46% on never-seen bearings. Healthy vs damaged is reliable; fault type and size are not.
    4. 
    
  4. Simulated decisions. The RL agent's world, costs and failure rates are assumptions. Its choices show the method, not a validated plant policy.
    5. 
    
  5. No edge hardware. Speeds measured on a laptop.
    6. 
    
  6. Rule-based health. Healthy / Warning / Critical comes from written rules, not from measurement.
    7. 
    
  7. Bundled samples were in training. The app detects this and warns that ~100% confidence is expected.
